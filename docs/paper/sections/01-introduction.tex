\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}

On 29 October 2024 a DANA — an isolated depression at high levels — struck the
province of Valencia, Spain. It killed 223 people, displaced roughly 15{,}000
residents and caused losses estimated above €50 billion. The Spanish
meteorological agency AEMET issued its highest-level red warning hours before
the worst of the rainfall, but the regional ES-Alert message to citizens'
mobile phones was not sent until late that evening. People died in their
homes, in underground garages, and in vehicles overtaken by water.

The gap that day was not a gap in forecasting. It was a gap between what was
known and what reached the people affected, and between a hazard beginning and
anyone confirming that it had begun. The 2021 eruption of Cumbre Vieja on La
Palma, which forced thousands to evacuate over 85 days, showed the same
pattern from a different hazard.

Spanish cities already carry tens of thousands of cameras: municipal traffic
cameras, road authority cameras, private security systems. During the DANA
those cameras were watching the water rise. Nobody was watching the cameras.

\subsection{What VIGÍA is}

VIGÍA integrates the best publicly available hazard detectors under a single
gated pipeline and adds a shared temporal validation layer that makes them
operationally trustworthy on ordinary municipal cameras.

The system is an integration project and does not claim novel detection
architectures. The validation layer itself is deliberately conventional —
confidence, IoU-tracked persistence, spatial cooldown, each individually
long-established — and the paper does not claim it as new. What is contributed
is the \emph{measurement}: the controls of \cref{sec:validator} that
establish, against a deployment-realistic baseline, what such a layer is
worth and where it is not; and the evaluation methodology of
\cref{sec:methodology} that keeps a small single-author system from
flattering itself. Every detector is credited by name, licence and source in
\cref{sec:attribution}.

\subsection{The problem the system actually solves}

Hazard detection models are widely available and, individually, good. What
prevents them from being deployed on municipal cameras is not accuracy but
trust: they alarm far too often. Industry reporting puts the proportion of
false alarms in security camera systems at roughly 98\,\%. Commercial wildfire
operators compensate with human staffing — Pano AI charges around
USD 50{,}000 per camera per year and runs a 24/7 intelligence centre, and
ALERTCalifornia routes detections from more than 1{,}060 cameras through
trained staff before dispatch.

We measured this directly instead of citing it. Run exactly as published, the
PyroNear wildfire detector~\citep{pyronear2025} raises an alarm on
\FireImageFAR{} of frames containing no smoke when evaluated against a curated
hard-negative set of \FireNegatives{} images spanning 21 cameras
(\cref{sec:fire}). The detector is not defective; that is the operating point
its authors deliberately chose, assuming a downstream filter. VIGÍA is that
filter.

\subsection{Preliminary conclusions}
\label{sec:preliminary}

Because this is a preprint offered for discussion, the conclusions it reaches
are stated here at the outset and revisited, with their qualifications, in
\cref{sec:conclusion}. In brief: a shared temporal validator makes published
hazard detectors materially more usable on municipal cameras, but its value
is narrower than a raw suppression figure suggests. On an easy negative set an
oracle-tuned confidence threshold matches the validator's false-alarm rate
more cheaply (\cref{sec:threshold-matched}); but a cross-camera transfer
experiment (\cref{sec:threshold-transfer-exp}) shows that a threshold chosen
the way a deployment must choose it — on some cameras, run on others — either
lets false alarms drift up or drops a detection, while the cascade holds both.
The validator's defensible advantages are therefore that its stability does
not depend on the deployment's negative distribution, that it deduplicates
alerts in a way no threshold can, and that one configuration transfers across
four hazards with different physics. Four candidate improvements were measured and three rejected on the
evidence, and every quantitative claim is bounded by the interval its sample
supports. These findings are preliminary in the precise sense that several
rest on small held-out samples, named individually in \cref{sec:outstanding};
the paper is written so that a reader can locate, and disagree with, each one.

\subsection{Research questions and contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This is a measurement-driven systems paper: its purpose is not a new
detection architecture but establishing, with controls, what a validation
layer over published detectors is worth. Three questions organise it:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.2em,style=sameline,font=\normalfont\bfseries]
  \item[RQ1] What does temporal validation buy over the trivial alternative —
        raising the detector's confidence threshold — and at what cost?
        (\cref{sec:validator}, especially the control in
        \cref{sec:threshold-matched}.)
  \item[RQ2] Does one untuned validation mechanism transfer across hazards
        with different physics, and can that property be \emph{enforced}
        rather than asserted? (\cref{sec:validator}, \cref{sec:architecture}.)
  \item[RQ3] Which evaluation practices stop a small single-author system
        from deceiving itself? (\cref{sec:metrics}, and the corrections
        recorded throughout.)
\end{description}

The contributions, stated so they can be checked:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A threshold-matched control and a cross-camera transfer
        experiment for alarm-suppression claims}
        (\cref{sec:threshold-matched}, \cref{sec:threshold-transfer-exp}):
        on easy negatives an oracle-tuned floor dominates our own cascade — a
        finding we publish against ourselves — but under the transfer
        conditions a real deployment faces, the untuned cascade holds its
        false-alarm rate and its recall where the tuned threshold holds at
        most one. We argue both controls should be standard in the genre;
        they are absent from the systems we compare against.
  \item \textbf{A measured decomposition of ``suppression'' into filtering
        and deduplication} (\cref{eq:decomposition}), which differ by a
        factor of eight between hazards; single suppression figures conflate
        two different claims.
  \item \textbf{A closed-form latency law for persistence},
        $\mathbb{E}[\Delta t] = (N-1)/f$, matching measurement exactly and
        making the recall/latency price of validation predictable before
        deployment (\cref{sec:latency}).
  \item \textbf{Negative results with mechanisms}: two cascade levels
        removed on evidence — one inverting the safety property on severe
        floods, one failing because fog is exactly as grey as smoke
        (\cref{sec:removed-levels}) — plus a register of the project's own
        corrected claims.
  \item \textbf{An enforcement architecture}: licence isolation, a
        no-biometrics boundary, viewpoint envelopes, figure provenance and
        the validator's hazard-agnosticism held by build-failing checks, so
        the paper's claims are continuously re-verified in public.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope and limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item VIGÍA is a research prototype, not a certified emergency system. No
        component has regulatory approval for life-safety use.
  \item It performs no biometric identification of any kind, by architecture
        not by configuration (\cref{sec:privacy}).
  \item Each detector carries a declared operating envelope, enforced before
        any model is loaded. Claims outside that envelope are not made.
  \item Several figures come from small samples. Each is reported with the
        interval its sample size supports (\cref{sec:metrics}), and the
        binding cases are listed in \cref{sec:outstanding}.
  \item One hazard — road incidents — is retained as a generality experiment
        for the validator, not a disaster capability, and has no
        standalone measurement against held-out labels.
\end{itemize}
